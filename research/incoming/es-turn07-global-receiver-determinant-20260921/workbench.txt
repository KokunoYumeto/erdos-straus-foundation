       Abstract. For the literal prime–denominator quartic of an integral Erdős–Straus witness,
       we prove strict negativity of the original odd-receiver determinant numerator at every prime
       p ≡ 1 (mod 12). Explicit universal constants give −N > cp8 and ∥O−1 ∥2 < 360S 9 /(cp8 ).
       The proof uses the complete Type I/II domain and two exact positive-coefficient polynomial
       certificates. This settles the arithmetic nonvanishing question isolated in the supplied programme
       audit, without its three-character restriction. It does not prove that an ES witness exists
       at every prescribed prime. Full integer coefficients, source maps, negative controls and two
       standard-library verification procedures are supplied.

                2. Original states and the complete first-half domain
   Here we give the elementary reductions needed to apply the theorem to every witness, rather
than assume that its distinguished denominator has been chosen in a restricted family. The Type
I/II framework is classical [1, §2, Propositions 2.2 and 2.6].
   Every denominator exceeds p/4. At least one is divisible by p, by clearing the reciprocal
equation; all three cannot be, since their reciprocal sum would then be at most 3/p. A denominator
equal to p would give
                                        (3y − p)(3z − p) = p2 .
Here y, z > p/3, so both displayed factors are positive. The positive factors of p2 are 1 (mod 3),
whereas each displayed factor is 2 (mod 3). Hence no denominator equals p. If two denominators
equal a, positivity of the third denominator gives d = 2a − p > 0. Then 4dz = p(d + p) and d | p2 .
The three possibilities d = 1, p, p2 give respectively z = p(p + 1)/4, p/2, (p + 1)/4, all nonintegral
for p ≡ 1 (mod 4). Thus all four literal roots are distinct.
   If two denominators are multiples of p, the third, denoted by a, must satisfy a < p/2: otherwise
the sum is at most 4/p, with equality requiring the impossible integer a = p/2. The other two
denominators exceed a.
   If exactly one denominator is pk, let the other two be a ≤ b, both units at p. The identity
                                      (4k − 1)ab = pk(a + b)
gives 4k − 1 = pt, where t ≡ 3 (mod 4) and gcd(k, t) = 1. Write a = gr, b = gs, r ≤ s,
gcd(r, s) = 1. Then tgrs = k(r + s), so k = drs, r + s = tλ and g = dλ: indeed gcd(rs, r + s) = 1
gives rs | k, say k = drs; then tg = d(r+s), and gcd(d, t) = 1 gives the remaining two divisibilities.
If a ≥ p/2, substitution of p = (4drs − 1)/t and a = dλr gives

again p/4 < a < p/2, and a is the unique smallest original denominator.
